ABSTRACT

2

Digital rock physics involves the modern microscopic imaging of geomaterials,

3

digitalization of the microstructure, and numerical simulation of physical properties of rocks.

4

This physics-based approach can give important insight into understanding properties of

5

reservoir rocks, and help reveal the link between intrinsic rock properties and macroscopic

6

geophysical responses. The complex conductivity of carbonate reservoir rocks was simulated

7

using reconstructed 3D rock structures from high-resolution X-ray micro computed tomography

8

(micro-CT). Carbonate core samples with varying lithofacies and pore structures from the

9

Cambro-Ordovician Arbuckle Group and the Upper Pennsylvanian Lansing-Kansas City Group

10

in Kansas were used in this study. The wide variations in pore geometry and connectivity of

11

these samples were imaged using micro-CT. A two-phase segmentation method was used to

12

reconstruct a digital rock of solid particles and pores. The effective electrical conductivity of the

13

digital rock volume was then calculated using a pore-scale numerical approach. The complex

14

conductivity of geomaterials is influenced by the electrical properties and geometry of each

15

phase, i.e., the solid and fluid phases. In addition, the electrical double layer that forms between

16

the solid and fluid phases can also affect the effective conductivity of the material. The influence

17

of the electrical double layer is quantified by a complex surface conductance and converted to an

18

apparent volumetric complex conductivity of either solid particles or pore fluid. The effective

19

complex conductivity resulting from numerical simulations were compared to results from

20

$
laboratory experiments on equivalent rock samples. In general, simulated 𝜎"##
values were

21

$$
below laboratory measurements, while numerical 𝜎"##
values were within reasonable range. The

22

imaging and digital segmentation technique, fundamental rock characteristics, and model

23

assumptions all play an important role in the simulation process.

INTRODUCTION

25

Macroscopic geophysical measurements are governed by pore-scale rock properties.

26

Understanding pore-scale structures and processes of rocks is necessary to fully interpret

27

laboratory and field geophysical observations. Current geophysical interpretations rely on

28

models that attempt to connect geophysical observables with geological properties of interest.

29

These models assume a simplified microstructure; however, real rocks are intrinsically

30

heterogeneous and complex, especially for difficult formations like shales and carbonates.

31

Carbonate rocks contain a significant amount of hydrocarbon resources and form many

32

important water-bearing zones. Our goal is to explore how pore-scale geometry of these

33

complicated rocks control the complex electrical conductivity, which can be used to reveal pore

34

structure as well as fluid-rock interactions in carbonate rocks in order to help us better

35

understand various geological processes. We achieve this goal by using emerging digital rock

36

physics techniques and previously defined electrochemical models that explain complex

37

conductivity in porous media.

38

Digital rock physics involves high-resolution 3D imaging of a real samples, digitization

39

of the microstructure, and computation of macroscopic properties (Andrä et al. 2013a, b). The

40

use of digital rock physics as a research tool has grown over the past few decades with the

41

increase in computing power. A wide range of studies exist for sandstones, shales, carbonates,

42

and unconsolidated material. Most of these studies focus on fluid transport and acoustic

43

attenuation (Knackstedt et al. 2009, Vanorio and Mavko 2011, Quintal et al. 2011) with a sizable

44

amount of studies on electrical conductivity (or resistivity) and formation factor (Yan et al. 2016,

45

Sengupta, Kittridge, and Blangy 2017) and even fewer incorporating the effects of the electrical

2

46

double layer and surface conductance (Shabro et al. 2014). In the nomenclature of geophysics,

47

digital rock physics takes the forward modeling approach to understand scientifically and

48

societally important topics such as contaminant transport, CO2 sequestration, and reservoir

49

characterization.

50

Our study entails the computation of the effective (bulk) complex conductivity of digital

51

carbonate reservoir rock volumes and a comparison to laboratory measurements. The complex

52

electrical response of geomaterials can be measured by the induced polarization (IP) method in

53

the field and laboratory (Kemna et al. 2012) and is influenced by the electrical properties of the

54

solid and fluid phases, the geometry of the microstructure, and the electrical double layer that

55

forms at the solid-fluid interface (Revil, Florsch, and Camerlynck 2014). The IP method is

56

especially sensitive to internal surface area, connectivity, and fluid chemistry. The interpretation

57

of laboratory or field data can lead to the estimation of pore size distributions (Florsch, Revil,

58

and Camerlynck 2014), contaminant plume mapping (Sogade et al. 2006), and microbial activity

59

monitoring (Davis et al. 2006). Our study specifically explores how pore-scale rock parameters

60

(i.e., surface charge density 𝑄 and dynamic pore radius 𝛬), pore size distributions based on

61

extracted pore network models, and micro-CT imaging resolutions can affect the simulated bulk

62

conductivity response of our digital rocks. We then compare the simulated values to spectral

63

induced polarization (SIP) laboratory results measured on the same samples.

CT scanning and reconstruction

82

Our study used X-ray micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) scanning technology to

83

obtain three-dimensional digital rock volumes. X-rays travel through the sample and refract

84

according to Snell’s law. The resulting image intensity is high (bright) in areas where x-rays are

85

converging, and vice-versa for low intensity (darker) areas. The detector measures the phase

86

contrast. In effect, brighter regions indicate harder rock parts, and darker regions indicate softer

87

rock parts and pores. This brightness contrast is the basis for identifying solid versus pore

88

regions during the segmentation process. We cut the core plugs into thin sticks (roughly 1.5 mm

89

– 3.5 mm thick) to prepare for micro-CT scanning. We used microscope lenses with two

90

different optical magnifications, 10X and 4X, to explore different resolutions over the samples

91

using the phase contrast micro-CT Xradia MicroXCT-400 microscope. Magnification levels and

92

resolutions are included in Table 1. There is a trade-off between the magnification level and the

93

effective volume that can be scanned; higher resolution scans cover a smaller 3D sample volume.

94

The microscope software outputs the raw data images as a sequence of 2D slices in grayscale.

95

The intensity value of each pixel ranges from 0 (darkest) to 255 (lightest). Roughly 1000 image

96

slices with dimensions of 1000 × 1000 pixels were acquired for each sample. A 600 × 600 pixel

97

area, centered on the axis of the sample, was then cropped out of each slice, and the 600 middle

98

slices were then compiled together sequentially to form a 6003 digital array using MATLAB.

5

99

The reconstructed digital volumes from micro-CT scanning revealed the varying levels of

100

diagenesis, different pore structures, and connectivity characteristics of our samples. Certain

101

cementation and harder material, most likely metallic minerals such as pyrite, were visible in

102

some of the samples. For example, sample G2 contained larger shell fragments and fossils.

103

Sample G6, a skeletal grainstone from Upper-Pennsylvania Lansing-Kansas, was characterized

104

by intergranular porosity and contained pellets, some of which were partly dissolved. There

105

appeared to be good connectivity surrounding the pellets and between some of the larger grains.

106

Most of our grainstones were oomoldic in nature and contained many large dissolved pores, but

107

some samples also contained a small amount of only partly dissolved ooids. The large spherical

108

pore spaces sometimes overlapped or connected via smaller cracks.

109

Phase segmentation

110

A two-phase thresholding method was used to segment the image cubes into solid and

111

pore regions. To do this, a grayscale value was chosen for each sample that distinguished a solid

112

voxel from a pore voxel. Because each sample has different hardness, density, and cementation

113

characteristics, there are slight differences in the micro-CT scanning results and therefore the

114

threshold was tailored for each sample. To help choose the ideal threshold, an intensity

115

histogram was analyzed for each sample to identify the darker regions (pores) from brighter

116

regions (solid). The first and second peaks indicate clusters of pore and solid intensity values,

117

and a threshold value is chosen between these peaks to partition the two phases. For quality

118

control, a raw CT slice is compared to a segmented slice to visually check that the solid-pore

119

boundary is maintained with the chosen threshold value. This value ranged from 105 to 145 for

120

our samples.

Post-processing
We then compressed, or re-gridded, each 6003 digital cube into a 2003 cube in order to

123

reduce computer demand and computation times but maintain the same effective sample volume.

124

To do this, each original cube was partitioned into smaller cubes of 27 voxels. Within each

125

smaller cube, we looked to see how many pore voxels were present out of 27. If this value was

126

above a user-given criterion number, then we defined a new single pore voxel. If there weren’t

127

enough pore voxels within the smaller cube, then we defined a new single solid voxel. In effect,

128

we decreased the total number of voxels to process for each image cube from 6003 voxels to 2003

129

voxels to expedite our later calculation for electric current flow. Ideally, the compression

130

criterion number is chosen so that the final porosity of the digital structure is comparable to the

131

helium porosity. A minimum compression criterion value of 3, which could be interpreted as a

132

straight path across each smaller partitioned cube, can be used to represent the most relaxed

133

option for this processing step. Higher numbers require more pores to be present within each

134

partitioned cube in order to re-grid to a single pore voxel. To investigate how our data

135

compression method affected sample porosity, connectivity, and electrical conductivity, we

136

generated multiple microstructures with different compression criterions for each sample ranging

137

from 10 to 3. A summary of the compression criterion numbers used for each trial in this study is

138

included in Table 2.

Connectivity analysis

140

We then tested if the fluid phase percolates through the microstructure of each image

141

cube. A digital sample is connected if a given phase percolates through microstructure, from one

142

cube face to the opposite face. A connected sample has at least one pathway that begins with a

7

143

cell on the cube edge and connects to the other edge through cells of the same phase. Because the

144

pore fluid and the pore-solid boundary are the conducting regions, we checked to see if the pore

145

phase was connected in our samples in all three directions. To do this, we used a burning

146

algorithm developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (Garboczi 1998)

147

(available at https://ciks.cbt.nist.gov/garbocz/manual/node59.html). For more information on the

148

algorithm, see Garboczi (1998). Only five out of the original twelve samples were tested as well

149

connected, having the pore phase percolating through the sample in at least two axis directions.

150

We continued our investigation of these five samples only. Their micro-CT reconstructions are

151

shown in Figures 2 – 6. These images show the original micro-CT reconstructions with 600

152

stacked images on each sample and a cube edge of ~ 2.6 mm.

173

We generated pore network models using an algorithm developed by the Imperial

174

College London to extract information on the pore size distribution and obtain pore network

175

models of our microstructures. This maximal ball algorithm works by filling the pore space with

176

spheres inscribed within the solid-pore boundary. Large spheres near the center of pore regions

177

become nodes. Chains of smaller spheres that connect larger nodes become cylindrical tubes

13

178

179

space and connectivity of each sample. From this simplified pore network, we were able to

180

181

Effects of image resolution and processing

345

Digital rock physics entails a collection of various methods and issues arising from the

346

chosen techniques. Choosing a simple intensity threshold may not be the best method, as

347

variations in mineral density and scanning resolution complicate intensity histograms. A kriging

348

segmentation method has been shown to be superior to simple thresholding (Sakellariou et al.

31

349

2003, Lindquist et al. 2000). A watershed segmentation method may also be used on high-

350

resolution images to better discern the solid-pore boundary (Saenger and Madonna 2011).

351

Simple intensity thresholding tends to cause separate grains to appear as one connected mass;

352

In addition to segmentation considerations, the scanning resolution and grid spacing must

361

362

363

364

365

366

367

368

Effects of pore-scale simulation parameters

404

In the simulation, a number of variables are used as inputs to model pore-scale rock

405

properties and the effects of the electrical double layer at fluid-rock interfaces. Some of these

406

parameters are difficult to measure experimentally and can only be perceived conceptually.

407

Determining these parameters is important because it helps us understand the macro-scale

408

electrical behavior of rocks and the issues with upscaling. In our study, we explored determining

409

pore size distributions using pore network models. We focused on the effects of the dynamic

410

pore radius 𝛬 and surface charge density 𝑄. The value of these parameters can significantly

411

impact simulation results.

412

Pore network modeling

413
414

The pore network models in our study were generated using the maximal ball algorithm
discussed in the “pore network extraction” section (Dong and Blunt 2009). This algorithm

34

415

generated the sizes and locations of pore nodes (spheres) and pore throats (tubes) encompassed

416

within the pore phase of the digital microstructure. The extracted network represents a simplified

417

model of the imaged pore space. Pore-scale flow simulations based on these maximal ball

418

models (Mahabadi et al. 2016) are typically performed instead of direct calculations on the

419

digital pore space as they are computationally faster. These simulations are able to predict

420

permeability and capillary pressure well, but can underestimate other features that were directly

421

calculated on the pore space (Dong and Blunt 2009). There is also some concern of whether or

422

not these maximal ball networks oversimplify the real structure by losing some details,

423

particularly the smaller-sized connectivity compared to the rest of the network (Blunt et al.

424

2013), which is particularly present in more complex samples such as carbonates.

425

In our study, the pore networks generated from the maximal ball approach helped to

426

quantify a pore size distribution for our samples. Pore size distributions are important to pore-

427

scale rock characterization and can be measured using laboratory methods such as mercury

428

injection capillary pressure (MICP) (Scott and Barker 2003, Niu and Revil 2015), SIP, and

429

nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) (Niu and Zhang 2018). The pore size distribution data

430

extracted from our pore network models was used to mathematically define pore sizes 𝑟B and

431

their respective weights 𝑓B . This information was carried through in the electrical double layer

432

and induced polarization modeling by affecting values for the radius of the curvature of the

433

electrical double layer (equated to 𝑟B ), the relaxation time 𝜏B , and the influence of the complex

434

surface conductance induced in the Stern layer 𝐶=9 . It is important to note our separate use of the

435

pore size distribution 𝑟B and the dynamic pore radius 𝛬. There are studies that do in fact equate

436

these two values (Revil, Florsch, and Camerlynck 2014, Niu and Revil 2015) and others that use

437

a grain size parameter when defining relaxation times (Revil, Koch, and Holliger 2012). Many of

35

438

these studies assume spherical grains, which may not be realistic for our more complex

439

carbonate samples. For our study, we wanted to obtain a representation of the pore size based on

440

image analysis, so we decided to extract this information from the maximal ball pore networks

441

and equate it to 𝑟B . This allowed us to characterize the general shape of the pore size distribution,

442

but as discussed earlier, much of the sub-resolution porosity is lost during the imaging process.

443

Therefore, we kept the 𝛬 parameter (discussed below) as a user-defined value in the simulation.

444

It could prove beneficial to supplement pore size estimates based on image analysis alone with

445

laboratory MICP measurements to more accurately represent the full distribution and hopefully

446

lead to a better prediction of the induced polarization effects. Pore network extractions on digital

447

carbonates rocks can be poorly connected due to the resolution issue (Dong and Blunt 2009), and

448

as discussed earlier, image-based methods will underestimate porosity (Sakellariou et al. 2003).

449

Therefore, we assume there is some degree of inaccuracy in representing these real values due to

450

resolution and processing limitations. This could explain why the simulated behavior of the

451

mudstone sample showed the greatest deviation from laboratory measurements, as there may be

452

considerable pore space below our resolution limits.

453

Dynamic pore radius 𝛬

454

The parameter 𝛬 is sometimes referred to as the “characteristic” pore size – it is linked to

455

the pore-scale geometry of the medium and to the conducting regions at the grain-fluid interface.

456

Studies on fluid flow in porous materials commonly make use of a hydraulic pore radius, defined

457

as 𝑉v ⁄𝑆 in Kozeny-Carmen equations, where 𝑉v is pore volume and 𝑆 is surface area. 𝛬 can be

458

defined as weighted version of the hydraulic pore radius, weighted by the norm of the electric

459

field in the pore space before the formation of the electrical double layer (Revil, Koch, and

36

460

Holliger 2012). It represents the radius of interconnected pores that control fluid and electrical

461

transport phenomena in porous media. Transport is dominant in large, connected pore throats. 𝛬

462

is inversely related to the formation factor 𝐹 and surface area-to-pore volume ratio 𝑆⁄𝑉v

463

(Johnson, Koplik, and Schwartz 1986). In the modeling, 𝛬 is included in the denominator of our

464

definition of the complex conductivity of the pore fluid, 𝛥𝜎7∗ . Therefore, a smaller 𝛬 will

465

translate to a larger induced complex conductivity. This inverse relationship exists because

466

smaller pores represent samples with a rougher texture and a higher surface area (Revil, Florsch,

467

and Camerlynck 2014) leading to more surface sites where charge build up can occur. Therefore,

468

rocks comprised of smaller pores will exhibit more significant polarization than those with larger

469

pores and of the same lithology. This is especially helpful in characterizing the differences in

470

complex rocks such as carbonates.

471

As mentioned earlier, the 𝛬 parameter can be obtained experimentally from MICP

472

measurements. We do not have MICP data, however for simulation purposes we substituted

473

values within a range previously measured for carbonate reservoir rocks (Oyewole, Saneifar, and

474

Heidari 2016, Duan et al. 2018). Only 𝛬 was altered for trials G2-A (𝛬 = 100 nm), G2-B (𝛬 =

475

Surface charge density 𝑄

486

The surface charge density 𝑄 is a physical concept that describes the amount of charge

487

buildup at the solid-fluid interface. It is affected by the mineralogy of the grain, the electrolyte

488

concentration, and the pH. It can be measured in the laboratory by potentiometric titrations and

489

confirmed by streaming zeta potential experiments (Revil and Glover 1997, Revil, Pezard, and

490

Glover 1999, Walker and Glover 2018). A higher 𝑄 implies a greater number of surface charge

491

sites, which enhances electrochemical interactions at the solid-fluid interface and strengthens the

492

polarization effect (Niu, Revil, and Saidian 2016, Niu and Revil 2015). In the modeling, a higher

493

𝑄 leads to an increased complex conductance in the diffuse layer 𝐶=. and Stern layer 𝐶=9 ,

494

ultimately producing a higher complex conductivity in the fluid phase voxels 𝛥𝜎7∗ . In our

495

modeling, we assumed that the charge densities associated with Stern and diffuse layers were

496

equivalent. To maintain electrical neutrality, the sum of 𝑄 from the Stern and diffuse layers

497

represents the total charge density on the mineral grain (Leroy et al. 2008). Details on the how 𝑄

498

relates to the sodium sorption can be found in the Appendix B of Niu, Revil, and Saidian 2016.
